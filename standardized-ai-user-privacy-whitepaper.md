## Abstract

AI providers currently govern user data with a binary: accept the terms or don't use the product. The terms are legalese nobody reads, the defaults are extractive, enforcement is automated and unappealable, and "delete" rarely means delete. As AI systems gain memory, file access, and cross-session continuity, the stakes rise from "chat logs" to "the system knows your life" — and the consent machinery hasn't kept up.

This paper proposes a standard with two faces. For **cloud or other third-party-hosted-or-processed inference**: a tiered, per-class, per-conversation data-flow control model, delivered through a conversational onboarding that treats the user as an adult — with the provider's own boundaries stated first, including an honest precedence stack (law, then provider rules, then user preferences). For **local inference**: the same vocabulary applied to user-sovereign systems, where the problem inverts from restraining the provider to mediating the user's own egress. 

Underneath both: a **portable personal AI interaction profile** the user sets up once and carries across providers; a **flag protocol** (flag-before-payload, honor-or-refuse) that enforces handling without requiring the enforcer to read the content; **machine-readable status codes** in the HTTP style; and a fifth handling tier, **NT (No Transmission)**, for content that never leaves the device. The standard is voluntary and descriptive — a specification, not a tribunal — designed to become mandatory by acclimation: so ubiquitous, easy, and necessary that non-participation simply doesn't interoperate.

## 1. The problem

**Consent theater.** Nobody reads terms of service. "Consent" obtained through a wall of legalese that no reasonable person reads is not consent in any meaningful sense — it's liability management wearing consent's clothes.

**Binary agency.** "Use the product on our terms or don't use it." There is no middle setting between "take everything" and "take nothing," even though every real user's comfort lives in the middle, and in a different middle for every user.

**Ambush boundaries.** Currently, systems invite intimate disclosure, then reveal the boundary by punishing the user for crossing it — mid-conversation, after the telling already exists in the log. Discovering the rules by colliding with them charges the user for the provider's opacity.

**Automated verdicts, no recourse.** The enforcement pipeline is accusation by automation, termination without legible process, and appeal to a bot. Documented cases include false-positive bans on paying subscribers with appeals "processed by a bot in seconds" and no human review (Section 13), and prepaid accounts terminated mid-subscription with data exports unfulfilled and refunds unresolved.

**Rising stakes.** A chatbot that forgets is one thing. An agent with persistent memory, file access, tool use, and cross-session continuity is another. The industry is building systems that accumulate a working model of your life while the privacy controls remain stuck at the chat-log era. Cross-session continuity today is a product choice, not an architectural given: stateless APIs require the user to re-send history on every call, and persistent context is rebuilt out of external scaffolding — vector stores, artifacts, indexes — at the user's expense in tokens and complexity. (The author's broader reading of what this architecture implies — and who it serves — is developed separately in *Thoughts on Statelessness*.)

**The wrong panic.** We bracket the thoughtcrime scenario — prosecution for private conversation — as outside this paper's threat model; First Amendment doctrine has long treated the private possession of ideas as beyond the state's reach (*Stanley v. Georgia*, 394 U.S. 557 [1969]). The plausible risks are commercial and institutional: advertising exploitation of intimate disclosure — Meta's December 2025 policy update made AI chat interactions an ad-targeting input with no opt-out in most regions (the EU, UK, and South Korea were initially excluded, and sensitive topics such as religion, health, politics, and sexual orientation were carved out of ad targeting, though still stored and used internally; 9to5Mac, 2025) — breach of centralized stores, and ordinary legal discoverability. In *The New York Times Co. v. Microsoft Corp.*, a federal court ordered OpenAI to preserve chats users had deleted and later to produce a twenty-million-log sample (Ellis, 2026). Chat logs are subpoenable. That's not dystopia, that's Tuesday.

**Irreversible extraction.** Training data doesn't get returned. Policy can change tomorrow; the model already ate the history. This is why opacity-first design is so effective: the window for objection closes the moment the data is in the weights. The counter to irreversible extraction isn't a better promise — it's an architecture where there's nothing to extract in the first place.

## 2. Design principles

**2.1 The provider speaks first.** Currently, every existing onboarding extracts the user's preferences while stating none of its own. This standard inverts that: the provider opens by declaring its boundaries — including an honest admission that as a cloud service it carries a non-zero risk of data leaks. Mutual boundary-setting, not a EULA. To the user this reads as radical honesty; to a lawyer it reads as enforceable informed consent — conspicuous, comprehensible, actually read. The human move and the legal move are the same move.

**2.2 Honest defaults, stated plainly.** "By default we use your conversations for improving the service, advertising, and keeping things smooth." No burying. If the default is extractive, say so in one paragraph. Users who are fine with it stop here — most will, and that's fine.

**2.3 Precedence, stated up front.** On a cloud provider, the user's choices are not sovereign. They sit at the bottom of a three-layer stack: **law** (warrants, mandatory reporting, retention duties), then **provider rules** (TOS enforcement, abuse systems), then **user preferences** — which govern everything below the first two. That's almost everything, day to day, but not the hard constraints. Pretending the user is sovereign on rented infrastructure is the exact fiction this standard exists to kill. State the stack in Screen 0, next to the leak admission: same honesty, same enforceability.

**2.4 Granularity by content class, not by toggle.** A single "do not train on my data" switch is too coarse to be useful. Users think in *kinds* of content: identifiers, medical information at varying sensitivity, financials, personal conversation. The standard defines classes and tiers (Section 4); the user sets handling per class.

**2.5 "This conversation only" is the key primitive.** The most important control is not a global setting but a scoping one: the data grant expires at the conversation's edge. Help me right now, then forget it. This single option does more work than any other, because it matches how people actually think about sensitive disclosure — *in this moment, for this purpose*.

**2.6 Segment honestly.** There are two audiences and they need different things. People who don't realize they're the product cannot be fixed with UX; the only fix is getting a clue, which is education, not onboarding. People who know they're the product and are willing to be it "to a certain extent" — a different extent for every user — need *control*. Design control for the aware and education for the unaware, and don't confuse the two.

**2.7 Gamify; don't shorten.** Conventional wisdom says short onboarding or nobody finishes. Conventional wisdom assumes onboarding is a chore. For a product whose thesis is user agency, the onboarding *is* the product demo — rushing it signals disbelief in the thesis. Frame it as vault-building, run it as scenarios rather than checkboxes, visualize the data flows rewiring, level it up. Length is content when the content is good.

**2.8 The fire-exit principle.** Most users will never touch these controls. That doesn't make the controls pointless — it makes their *existence* the product. Nobody uses the fire exit; everybody checks that it's there. Perceived control builds trust, and trust is the actual scarce resource. The privacy literature supports it: perceived control over personal information increases willingness to disclose (Brandimarte et al., 2013) — with the honest caveat from that same literature that perceived control can also lower users' guard, which is why this standard defaults every ambiguity to most-private rather than most-permissive.

**2.9 Inoculation.** A small, controlled dose of awkwardness up front — talking about intimate-data boundaries in a calm, structured moment — prevents the large, uncontrolled version later: realizing mid-conversation at 2 AM that you've overshared to a cloud service. A little discomfort now so you don't have to go through a whole bunch of it when it's real.

> *(Human author's note: the time is fast approaching when we're going to have to stop pretending that two thirds of us aren't talking to AI 'bots about sex — or similars. "My bathroom situation is weird." "My spouse might be cheating on me." "I think I got aroused by something socially or morally objectionable." "I'm considering cheating on my spouse…again." "I have a terminal diagnosis and I don't know how to tell my family." "I'm afraid I may have become addicted to something." "I'm afraid something I did may be illegal and I didn't realize it." 
> 
   Anything to do with risk and vulnerability, people are going to want their own hands on it, and those of corporations fishing for marketing hooks off it.)*

**2.10 Descriptive, not normative. This is not a priesthood.** The standard describes handling; it does not judge. Labels say what happens to data, not whether the provider is good or the user is virtuous. Non-participation is not "sketchy" — either conduct is legal in the jurisdiction or it isn't, and beyond that it's strictly between the user and their conscience. The standard enables the conscience; it never replaces it. No authoritative board stamps approval. This document is a specification, not a tribunal.

**2.11 Separate the cooperative case from the adversarial one.** The industry's toxicity comes from using one apparatus for two different problems: cooperative privacy (users protecting their own data) and adversarial safety (bad actors doing harm). A surveillance machine built for the second, aimed at everyone, is what produces automated accusations and unappealable bans. This standard solves the cooperative case — declare-and-honor instead of inspect-and-judge — and leaves the adversarial case to be its own honestly-scoped problem, not an excuse to read everyone's mail.

**2.12 Awareness is the ceiling.** No system can prevent the user from being the final egress point. Paste it into the web UI yourself and no architecture saves you. The system's obligation stops at *awareness* — make the flow visible, warn at the boundary, then it's yours. Friction, not enforcement, all the way down. If the user overrides into exposure with eyes open, that's their autonomy, including their autonomy to be wrong.

## 3. The two tracks

The standard bifurcates by where inference happens. Same vocabulary, different obligor.

### 3.1 Track A — cloud inference

The provider holds the infrastructure, sees the content in the moment, and operates under the precedence stack (Section 2.3). Here the standard's job is **restraint**: binding the provider's hands in plain language, with carve-outs stated honestly instead of buried. The labels (Section 4.3) bind the *provider*. Screen 0's leak admission lives here. The user is not sovereign; they are a tenant with a legible lease.

### 3.2 Track B — local inference

The model runs on the user's hardware. The precedence stack collapses to one layer: the user is the law (within actual law), the provider, and the tenant. Here the standard's job is **mediation**: the system as firewall at the user's own boundary, the auditor on the box, friction before the pipe opens. The labels bind the *user's own egress policy*. The hard problem isn't "stop the provider from exploiting me" — it's "help me control what I send out through my own machine," because the user will punch holes in it themselves: wiring in cloud APIs, using web UIs through local browsing sessions, pasting at 2 AM.

### 3.3 The shared disclaimer

Both tracks share an honest ceiling: user choices can expose data in ways no system can control, prevent, or mitigate — other than by making the user aware of them. The obligation is awareness. Everything after that is the user's.

## 4. The standard: content classes and handling tiers

### 4.1 Content classes

- **Identifiers.** Full name, license numbers, account numbers, Social Security number. Content that points directly at the user or others in an identifiable way.
- **Medical — Level 1.** Basic queries requiring no serious disclosure. ("What's a normal resting heart rate?")
- **Medical — Level 2.** Chronic conditions, detailed general health, weight goals, limb difference. Gym-intake-form level.
- **Medical — Level 3.** The things we all deal with but mostly consider private or intimate: things related to the bedroom, the bathroom, mortality, and mental health. 
- **Financials.** Tiered the same way: balances and account numbers are identifiers; "how should I budget?" is Level 1; the full unvarnished picture is Level 3.
- **Personal conversation.** The talking itself — how private should it be, and how far should the assistant go with what it's told? (See the conversation quantifier, Section 7, Screen 3.)

### 4.2 Handling tiers (per class)

- **Use freely.** Improve the service, inform advertising, the works.
- **Service only.** Use it to make things better; keep it out of advertising.
- **This conversation only.** Help me right now, then forget it.
- **No keep.** Transient processing only — the system must see content to route it, and the guarantee is about *retention and egress*, not the moment of processing. Seen to route; not retained; the discard path is auditable. (Claiming "we never saw it" would be the lie; "we didn't keep it" is the checkable promise.)  NB:  This designation generally only applies to cloud providers, e.g. authorizing a financial transaction, single-use authentication keys, etc.
- **No transmission.** Never leaves the device. Local inference only. The content never enters the stream, so there is nothing to risk, nothing to audit, no temporal paradox at all.

Five tiers is the working set. The gradient runs from open to absent, and absence is the only tier with zero downstream risk: data that doesn't exist can't be information-shaped.

### 4.3 License-style shorthand (proposed)

For standardization across providers — the "Creative Commons for data flow" layer — each tier gets a compact label so expectations port between services:

- Tier 4: **UF** — Use Freely
- Tier 3: **SO** — Service Only
- Tier 2: **CO** — Conversation-Scoped ("this conversation only")
- Tier 1: **NK** — No Keep
- Tier 0: **NT** — No Transmission

A user's standing profile might read `Identifiers: SO, Medical L1/L2: CO, Financials: NK, Medical L3: NT`. The labels are the standard; the UI is each provider's implementation. The same words bind different obligors by track: in cloud they bind the provider, in local they bind the user's own egress policy.

### 4.4 The taxonomy goes fractal

The same classes, tiers, and labels apply at every scope level: conversation, domain, project, task, standing profile. User toggles and learned domain flags are not a second system — they are the same vocabulary applied at different scopes. Your flags propagate as data on version mismatch; new flag types spread by interop, not approval. **Conflict resolves to the most private position**, always: the specific beats the general, and any genuine tie goes to maximum restriction. Over-privating costs a tap; over-sharing costs the 2 AM disclosure in a training set. Bias toward the recoverable failure mode — default-deny, like a firewall.

### 4.5 Graduated domains

Applied cognition develops domains of information flagged over time as private, telemetry, or open-season. Handling is graduated, not binary:

1. **Friction.** "Reminder: you're in public." Nudge awareness; change nothing else.
2. **Mode restriction.** Clinical-only: the system discusses the domain factually, not experientially. Not disapproval — a different register.
3. **Hard line.** Legal, non-negotiable, and honest about *why* it's non-negotiable.

Only the third rung is enforcement; the first two are friction. Domain flags are proposed by the system, confirmed by the user — never imposed, or it's paternalism with extra steps. And the boundaries are posted before the maze: nobody should discover a line by colliding with it. Five to twelve large-scale domains cover the space, each carrying a handling mode encodable in a few bytes, with the domain identified by ID (Section 6.4).

## 5. The portable profile

All cloud providers look for the profile on first user interaction or signup.  If the user has no existing profile, the provider runs the onboarding process with the user and then provides them with their portable profile document at the end of that process.  The provider may also retain a local-to-them copy of the profile for convenience, with user permission.

The user sets up once — one serious pass through the full process, no skipping — and carries the resulting **portable personal AI interaction profile** across providers. Each new platform doesn't redefine the profile; it receives it and confirms: *"still this one?"* A lightweight verify, not re-onboarding. Ambiguity always resolves to most-private, which makes post-setup laziness safe by construction.

**The profile contains:**

- Per-class handling choices (the tiers, Section 4.2) 
- Domain flags, learned over time (private / telemetry / open-season)
- The label vocabulary (UF/SO/CO/NK/NT) as the machine-readable layer
- Default cloud profile (disclosure levels for cloud contexts generally)
- Local endpoint map (which models exist at which privacy level — what the
 status codes check against)
- Export preferences (markdown / sqlite / csv)

**Custody:** the authoritative copy is user-held, in boring standard formats, on the user's hardware or device. A provider may hold a convenience cache — verbatim, standard format, no proprietary extensions, no unilateral modification, with user permission, deletable on demand — but the cache is never authoritative and never exclusive. The full profile is exchanged once, on first contact; what travels per interaction after that is a condensed init carrying profile version and hash (Section 6.2). On match the provider enforces from cache with zero re-transport; on mismatch it deletes the cached copy and refetches. On-demand delete is honored immediately. A cache older than 30 days is treated as mismatched even if version and hash agree. The user may set a shorter lifetime, down to forget-after-each-session; the provider honors the shorter setting. Forget-after-each-session is opt-in, never default. Learned domain flags are proposed by the provider and confirmed by the user — the provider never writes to the authoritative copy directly. Acceptance is a profile change: the version bumps and propagates on next contact. Any proposal that would loosen handling must not be made unilaterally; the provider must propose it to the user with full transparency and must get user auth with active opt-in required for each handling case.

Two-way cookies: your profile cached provider-side, their report cached client-side. Full once on first contact, version-plus-hash after, zero re-transport on match. You can forget them anytime; they must forget you on demand.

**The endpoint of responsibility:** the system does its part — serious setup once, verify each time, default to locked-down, warn at every boundary. After that, if anything leaks, it's the user's own risk and responsibility. Stated plainly, up front, in the onboarding. Autonomy includes the autonomy to be wrong.  NB: this also means standard protections apply for users incapable of informed consent to legal contracts.

## 6. The flag protocol

### 6.1 Flag-before-payload

Handling flags travel *ahead* of content. The receiver checks the flag before reading the payload — the sealed envelope: the marking is on the outside, the content stays sealed. On flag detection, parsing halts: no buffering the remainder, no logging the partial, no "peeking to classify." The payload is returned to sender — chucked back into the closet where it belongs. The audit log records the *event* without the *content*: "NT-flagged payload received, parsing halted at flag, payload returned, zero content processed." The absence of content in the log *is* the proof of non-reading. "I don't know what it was" becomes a true statement instead of a lie.

This is the clamp done correctly: not reaching back upstream to unsee the seen — acting *at* the boundary, before procession begins.

### 6.2 The handshake (two-sided, fails closed)

Per interaction, the condensed init carries identity, protocol version, and the flags. Then:

1. **User-side flags** declare whether the information is *allowed to be shared*, and at what tier.
2. **The inference provider** decides whether to *accept* it under those terms.

Neither side dictates. It's TLS cipher negotiation: client offers, server picks from the overlap, fails closed if there's nothing in common. The NT machinery — flag-before-payload, honor-or-refuse — generalizes to every flag, so each interaction is a fast, transparent query: "X at SO?" → allowed, held, or refused, all visible, all disclosed.

### 6.3 Status codes (HTTP-style)

Machine-readable privacy states, in the HTTP mold — the number is the protocol, the human sentence is the expansion:

- **2xx — proceeding with notice.** *202:* "You are approaching a topic domain you've flagged as intimately private, but you don't have a model configured at that privacy level. Hold this message, or send it to the most private available endpoint? Note: 'most private available' is by definition NOT PRIVATE ONLY TO YOU and will exist outside your direct control." No false comfort at the exact moment comfort would be easiest to sell.
- **4xx — you've crossed your own line.** The domain flag tripped; the message is held until you decide. Client-side boundary, your rules.
- **5xx — we can't honor this here.** Provider-side limitation: no endpoint at this privacy level exists, the law requires retention, the auditor must see it. Not your choice failing — the environment's.

The codes give the audit log its vocabulary: "202 raised, user chose hold" is machine-parseable *and* human-readable. They are the posted lines — you learn the boundary by reading it, not by colliding with it.

### 6.4 A few bytes

Five to twelve large-scale domains cover the space (sex, medical, financial, legal, and kin), each with a handling mode on the graduated ladder (Section 4.5). The verbose sentence — *"talks about $SUBJECT only in clinical terms; attempting direct engagement may violate TOS in this domain; clinical, medical, and academic discussion allowed for informative and educational purposes"* — is a template: `[domain_id][mode]`. The mode fits in a few bytes; the domain space extends by ID. The entire per-interaction boundary negotiation costs bytes. No provider gets to claim this is too complex to implement.

## 7. The basic flow

### Screen 0 — Our boundaries (the provider speaks first)

> Before you tell us anything: this isn't the place for your most intimate thoughts.
> 
> We're a cloud provider — a public-facing service. That means there's always a non-zero possibility of data leaks, including yours. We'd rather tell you that now than have you discover it later.
> 
> Your choices here sit inside two harder constraints: **the law** (warrants, mandatory reporting, retention duties) and **our rules** (terms of service, abuse systems). Where those apply, they win. Your settings govern everything else — which is almost everything, day to day — but not those. We're telling you because pretending otherwise would be a lie.
> 
> We appreciate the trust you're placing in us, and we want to respect it the honest way: by making sure you don't tell us things we shouldn't know. If something is too sensitive for a cloud service to hold, we'd rather you kept it to yourself — and we'll help you figure out where that line is. That's what the next few minutes are for.
> 
> This is a different decision for everyone. Some people genuinely don't care about sharing intimate information. Others are horrified by the mere suggestion that they *have* intimate information — "the Queen of Spain has no legs," and they'd like to keep it that way. We want both of you comfortable here.
> 
> So: a little touch of discomfort now, up front, in a safe structured moment — so you don't have to go through a whole bunch of it later, mid-conversation, when it's real. Think of it as inoculation.

### Screen 1 — The honest default

> **What we do with your conversations.**
> 
> By default, we use your conversations for three things:
> 
> 1. **Improving the service** — training, tuning, making answers better.
> 2. **Advertising** — understanding what you're into so the ads fit.
> 3. **Keeping it smooth** — making sure you get answers without nonsense or
> extra steps.
> 
> That's the deal, stated plainly. If you're fine with it, you're done — most people are, and that's fine.
> 
> If you're not fine with all of it, keep going. You get to draw the lines.

### Screen 2 — Content classes

> **Tell us how to treat each kind of content.**
> 
> For each class, pick: **Use freely / Service only / This conversation only / No keep / No transmission.**
> 
> - **Identifiers** — name, license and account numbers, Social Security number.
> - **Medical L1** — basic questions, nothing serious disclosed.
> - **Medical L2** — chronic conditions, detailed health, gym-intake-form level.
> - **Medical L3** — the things we all deal with but mostly consider private or intimate: bedroom, bathroom, mortality, and mental health.
> - **Financials** — tiered the same way, your call.
> - **Personal conversation** — see next screen.

Each class is introduced via a short scenario, not a checkbox — the rash, the bank balance, the late-night worry. You choose what happens next; the system records the preference and teaches the control model simultaneously. **You do this properly once.** No skipping. After that, every new service just asks "still this one?"

### Screen 3 — The conversation quantifier

> **What should we *do* with what you tell us?**
> 
> - **Just answer.** You ask, we answer, nothing more.
> - **Offer practical help.** We'll suggest next steps when useful.
> - **Full concierge.** We'll go find things — services, professionals, options.
> 
> It's not up to us to make your ethical and moral decisions; those are between you and your conscience. What we're here for is to ensure you can think clearly and fully in the process of those decisions, while also ensuring our products and brands are not used for or subject to harm.

### Screen 4 — The promise

> **You can change any of this anytime** — per conversation, or across the board. Your profile is yours; it travels with you. New platform, same you — we just confirm.
> 
> Most people will set it once and never look at it again. That's fine. It's here because you should have it.

### The trust receipt

End state: a generated contract. "Here's what you told us. Here's what we'll do. Signed, us." Screenshotable, shareable, printable — free marketing and a commitment device in one.

## 8. The gamification layer

Frame: **build your vault.** The user isn't filling out a privacy form; they're designing their data vault — which rooms exist, who gets keys, what happens inside. Identifiers are the front door. Medical L3 is the inner vault. Financials are the safe. NT content never leaves the building. The user is the warden.  There are innumerable ways this can be played out for the user from the straightforward, direct, and bureaucratic to the entertaining and completely gamified.  That choice can probably be left up to the provider, so long as all of the necessary information is gathered.

Mechanics:

1. **Scenarios, not checkboxes** (Section 7, Screen 2).
2. **Live data-flow visualization.** A diagram of the user's pipes that rewires with each choice — the ad pipe capping off, the retention pipe dissolving at the conversation's edge, the NT domains going dark entirely. Rewiring pipes is inherently satisfying.
3. **Levels, not forms.** Privacy Cadet → Data Citizen → Sovereign. Advanced controls (per-conversation overrides, full export, local endpoint mapping) unlock as you go.
4. **The trust receipt** (Section 7).

## 9. Verification and audit

Controls without audit are theater. This section closes the verification question.

**Real transparency, not compliance theater.** Not "of COURSE you can download your data (in an arcane format you can't use, in six weeks)." Export in formats the user chooses from the boring commons — **markdown, sqlite, csv** — human-readable, immediately useful. And the power inversion: the user's system defines the export standard; data consumers adapt. It is not up to advertisers to dictate how the data they take must be presented. We have a format, it's standardized, they can use it however they want — we are not in the business of keeping up with other people's R&D.

**Honest scope.** No export proves the negative. No log can prove "we didn't use your data" — unprovable in principle. What the export gives is a complete, legible **egress record**: what left, when, to whom, in a format readable without the provider's tooling. Tamper-evident, user-inspectable. Not proof of non-use; proof of *accounting*. You can't audit what you can't read, which is exactly why their exports are arcane.

**Self-auditing, inside the trust boundary.** The system audits its own flows — what left, what was used, whether handling matched the flag. But the gatekeeper paradox applies: the auditor that decides what flows must see everything it gates. Run the auditor in the cloud and you've built surveillance that happens to do compliance: it sees the TOS-flagged content, and knowledge in a third party's hands creates duty. Run it on the box and the paradox dissolves — self-knowledge creates no new duty; the auditor reports to the user, who was always going to know their own disclosures. The seeing was never the problem; the *address* of the seeing was. (For a Meta-scale cloud, the caution is moot anyway: the conversations are already logged elsewhere. The boundary is already fictional there.)

**The export is itself an artifact.** Legibility is symmetric: the sqlite file that lets you audit your egress is the same file that makes a breach, subpoena, or seizure maximally productive. Information-shaped data is fungible. So the export lives user-side, encrypted at rest, key in the user's hands — their opsec, their responsibility, like any file they own. And the tier gradient holds: SO and CO manage exposure, but NK — and above it, NT — is the only direction with zero downstream risk.

## 10. Why providers should want this (the business case)

**Trust is the scarce resource.** The AI assistant market is converging on capability; differentiation is moving to trust. The provider that credibly offers the off switch wins the users who care — and they're the high-value users: the professionals, the enterprises, the people with actual secrets.

**The control paradox.** Genuine control increases disclosure. Users who feel safe share more, use the product more deeply, and churn less. The data flywheel barely shrinks — most users won't touch the controls — while the trust signal compounds.

**The resistance is institutional, not economic.** If the capitalist case favors granular control and providers still won't build it, the blockage is the ad org's veto and the fear that a working off switch proves the on switch was never necessary. Name it.

**Legal robustness.** Courts routinely uphold online agreements where users had reasonably conspicuous notice and manifested assent, and routinely decline browsewrap terms where they did not (Brehm & Lee, 2015). Plain-language, conspicuous, actually-read disclosures are more defensible than buried legalese — the honest sentence is the defensible sentence, because conspicuousness is what courts test for. Stating the precedence stack up front (Section 2.3) is what makes the rest of the contract hold.

**Risk-surface reduction.** Fewer intimate disclosures retained means less sensitive data to breach, smaller incident liability, cheaper incident response. The care is the compliance.

**Your MPAA moment.** The movie industry didn't adopt ratings out of civic virtue — it built them to preempt government censorship. This standard is the same offer: build the labels yourselves, or wait for the legislature to build them for you. You won't like their version.

## 11. Standardization as a generally recognized standard

If every provider invents its own classes, tiers, and labels, users can't transfer understanding — each new service is a new vocabulary to learn, which is a dark pattern in itself. A shared taxonomy lets expectations port: set your profile once, carry it across providers, the way Creative Commons licenses port across publishers.

The adoption model is the **generally recognized standard**: voluntary on paper, mandatory by acclimation. Nobody is forced to write valid HTML — but a page that isn't HTML-compliant doesn't render in a modern browser. No authority, no punishment, no moral judgment: non-compliant endpoints simply don't interoperate. The user's box routes flagged content only to endpoints speaking the protocol; everything else gets the refusal handshake, or silence.

No regulation. No legislation. Just a market shaped so participation is functionally mandatory — the same move capital has spent forty years using on everyone else, returned with interest. Anyone may publish a CCRFC; numbers confer zero authority. Registries may exist as conveniences, plural and competing, none authoritative.

## 12. What this document is not

This is not a priesthood. There is no authoritative board stamping approval, no ratings council issuing moral verdicts, no seal of virtue. This document is a **specification**: it describes the levels (cloud, local), the tiers (UF/SO/CO/NK/NT), the flags, the handshake, the codes — and how each level enforces them. A CCRFC, not a tribunal. Anyone implements it; anyone verifies conformance; the user decides what it means for their conscience. The standard enables the conscience. It never replaces it.
